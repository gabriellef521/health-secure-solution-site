Don't Just Pick the Cheapest Premium: How to Compare Health Plans This Open Enrollment

Blog / Comparing Plans

By Gaby Fafard, Licensed Health Insurance Advisor · Fort Lauderdale, FL

When open enrollment comes around, most people sort plans by price and pick the lowest number. It's an understandable move, but the monthly premium is only part of what you'll actually pay. Here's how to compare plans so you don't get surprised later.

The Premium Is Only Part Of The Cost

Your premium is what you pay every month to keep the plan active, whether or not you use it. On top of that, you pay your share of care when you actually use it. A plan with a low premium often means you pay more out of pocket when you need care, and a plan with a higher premium often means you pay less.

Learn Four Terms

Deductible. The amount you pay for covered care before the plan starts sharing the cost (some services are covered before you meet it).

Copay and coinsurance. A copay is a flat fee, like for a doctor visit. Coinsurance is a percentage of the bill you pay after the deductible.

Out-of-pocket maximum. The most you'll pay in a year for covered, in-network care. After you reach it, the plan covers covered care at 100% for the rest of the year.

Network. The doctors, hospitals and pharmacies the plan works with. Going outside it can cost you a lot more, or not be covered at all.

A Simple Example

Say Plan A costs $300 a month with a $7,000 deductible, and Plan B costs $450 a month with a $2,000 deductible. Plan A is $1,800 cheaper over the year in premiums. But if you end up needing a major procedure, you could owe thousands more under Plan A before coverage really kicks in. (This is a simplified example with made-up numbers. Real plans have copays, coinsurance and other details.) If you rarely use care, the cheaper plan may be the right call. If you have ongoing prescriptions, a planned surgery, or kids who need regular visits, the higher-premium plan can come out ahead.

Check Your Doctors And Prescriptions

A plan can look great on paper and still not cover the doctor you love or the medication you take every day. Look up each doctor and each prescription before you choose, and do it again every year, because networks and drug lists can change.

Don't Just Let It Auto-Renew

If you do nothing during open enrollment, you'll generally be re-enrolled automatically, often in the same or a similar plan. But prices, networks and what's covered can change from one year to the next, and so can your income and your needs. Taking an hour to compare before you roll over can save you real money.

Know Your Deadlines

Open enrollment for 2027 coverage runs November 1 through January 15. To have coverage start January 1, select a plan by December 15. Enrolling after that, through January 15, means your coverage starts February 1.

The short version: don't pick on price alone. Look at the deductible, the out-of-pocket maximum, your doctors and your prescriptions, and compare more than one option before you decide.
